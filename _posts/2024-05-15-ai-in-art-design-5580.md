From January through May 2024, I took **DSGN 5580: AI in Art — Redefining Creativity in the 21st Century** with Professor Lisa Park. It was a hands-on studio at Penn where we engaged visual and multimedia AI tools as part of our individual artistic practice — and spent just as much time interrogating what *creativity*, *authorship*, and *craft* mean when the tools generate, remix, and respond on their own.

What follows is a week-by-week journal of what I learned, made, and kept thinking about.

## Week 00 — Course registration

I just snagged the last seat in this AI design studio class at Penn — so incredibly excited.

I've been stunned by the sheer number of AI-related classes being offered across Penn right now: journalism, art, policy, business, marketing, and of course computer science. Professors are adapting quickly to the changing landscape, and you can feel it in the catalog.

It's pretty unconventional for a PhD student at Penn's School of Medicine to enroll in an art & design class, but I'm grateful to my mentors for letting me chase down my curiosities. The purpose of Professor Park's new course is to let students experiment with a wide range of AI tools and expand our artistic practice. The syllabus sounds incredible.

## Week 02 — Evolution of AI in Art

First we dove into the social impact of AI. So many of us grew up watching sci-fi movies about AI, robots, and machines. For years, most people suspected those depictions were pretty far from reality — but in recent years, the emergence of AI has sent shockwaves across all of our lives. Not only in society, but also in art and design.

A few of the films we discussed:

- *Ex Machina* (2014)
- *Black Mirror* Season 2, Episode 1 (2013)
- *Her* (2013)
- *Coded Bias* (2020)

One of the *Black Mirror* episodes is about a service that uses AI to replicate a person's online personality based on their social posts, interactions, and text messages. A woman uses it to bring back a digital version of her deceased boyfriend — and the emotional arc is brutal.

We also looked at real-world examples:

- The **Replika** chatbot
- *Love and Loss in the Age of AI* about Joshua Barbeau, whose AI chatbot was modeled after his fiancée who had died ten years earlier
- **Rosanna Ramos**, who married an AI chatbot in 2023
- *The Rise of AI Companions* documentary (2022)
- *How This Holographic Anime Character Could Be Your Next Girlfriend* (2017)

It's wild to see how much compute has accelerated to land us here. We briefly touched on Ray Kurzweil's concept of the **Singularity**.

<!-- IMAGE: Kurzweil singularity diagram or slide -->

The coolest part was when Prof Park walked us through the **evolution of AI in art**, starting with the definition of art itself. Art used to be associated with certain skills, craftsmanship, and beauty — and then modern movements started challenging all of that.

We went era by era:

- **14th–16th century:** depicting reality and symbolism. The artist's role was record-keeper and storyteller, painting historical events and religious belief.
- **17th–18th century:** landscapes and portraits.
- **Late 19th–early 20th century:** the **invention of the camera in 1816** broke things wide open. Artists no longer had to do the labor-intensive job of "capturing" — they started exploring new ways of representing reality, focusing on emotion and subjective interpretation.
- **Mid-20th century** (one of my favorites): exploration of materials and conceptual art. Think **Marcel Duchamp**. Think controversial, sensational work where the *idea* behind the artwork carries more weight than the physical object.
- **21st century:** the incorporation of technology and digital media. Maybe the artist's role now is to integrate new tools and media into their creative process? There are obvious challenges — troubleshooting software/hardware, needing interdisciplinary teams instead of a single lone artist — but globalization and multiculturalism also open the door to a wider range of styles, themes, and lived perspectives.

We spent the rest of class trying entry-level AI image generation tools: **ChatGPT-4**, **Bing Create**, **Adobe Firefly**, **Photoshop Beta Generative Fill**, **DALL-E**. By the end of the session, we'd each created our first generative AI images. We also went around the room and shared takeaways from two assigned articles.

We each spent a few minutes ironing — warming the bag, pressing the design — and tada: my tote turned out *gorgeous*. I loved seeing what my classmates had made.

## Week 04 — Copyright Issues & Deepfakes in AI

On Valentine's Day, we each took turns sharing our comparisons of AI visual tools. Notes I took for future me:

- **Galileo** — good for UX mobile/app prototype design
- **Genie** — text-to-3D model generation, but kind of difficult and needs heavy refinement
- **Luma AI** (via Discord) — can produce cool 3D GIFs of mockups, though output isn't great for detailed products
- **Stable Diffusion** (via VSCode) — generates images in a virtual environment using Python commands
- **Veras AI** — requires an input image, then will fill in details and material choices for the desired output
- **Leonardo** and **Midjourney** — the friendliest interfaces

For my own piece this week, I worked on recreating idyllic village scenes from my Vietnamese seaside hometown using **Canva AI**, **Bing Create**, and **Leonardo AI** — including fine-tuning my own small image set with Leonardo.

## Week 05 — Field trip to the ICA (Institute of Contemporary Art)

Our class got a private tour with the curators of the ICA. Am I dreaming?

They walked us through every intentional curatorial decision in each exhibition. Right at the front entrance, we were told to look up at the entryway glass — custom window-pane designs commissioned from a Black artist from New Haven who draws on influences from Zimbabwe and the wider African continent. The designs featured 2D representations of wrought iron, drawing on the history of how enslaved blacksmiths once melted metal to forge physical wrought iron fences.

<!-- IMAGE: walk-through with ICA curators / entryway glass -->

One of the curators told us that curating is about bringing in artists whose medium is appropriate to the space and helps bring it alive. At the ICA specifically, the mission is to support up-and-coming artists, including helping them understand that artists *are* small businesses — and that the institution has a responsibility to help them figure out what they can and can't do in any given space or show.

We walked through each exhibit and got asked probing questions about color, material, and what we thought the artists were symbolizing.

## Week 07 — Spring Break (working through it)

It's Spring Break! But I got assigned one of the early midterm presentation slots right after we get back, so I have to figure out how to work on my AI art project from another state for the entire week.

I spent the week researching an old artisan handicraft practice: weaving **cói mats** from tall sedge grass.

### Learning about Vietnamese sedge mat weaving

Growing up, our own sedge mat was the center of a lot of activity in our home. These mats are massive — their surface area can cover a whole room, and they're used for gathering, eating together, or sleeping on.

Many cultures have their own version: the Cambodian **kantael**, the Japanese **tatami**, the Korean **wanchojang**.

### Building my own makeshift loom

Inspired by these rural village weavers, I wanted to try my hand at weaving my own mat to serve as a canvas for my AI images. I couldn't grow, harvest, dye, and dry my own sedge grass, so I had to think about what else could create a similar textured surface.

I remembered seeing sleeping mats woven from plastic grocery bags — and there are community efforts to make these for folks experiencing homelessness. Grocery bags are abundant. That decided it.

I wanted to use proper wood and nails for the loom, but with the time crunch I went with what was on hand: enough free cardboard boxes to assemble loom frames.

<!-- IMAGE: cardboard loom + plastic bag weaving setup -->

What shocked me was how *long* it took to weave a single small mat — one to two days. The master weavers in Vietnam can finish a massive mat in roughly 1.5 hours, working as a team of two or three.

## Weeks 08 & 09 — Midterm presentations

The first round of AI-generated midterm projects was mind-blowing. I cannot get over the level of creativity, originality, and intentionality my peers brought to their projects. That's the wild thing about both artists and AI tools — neither ever creates the same thing twice.

A few that stuck with me:

- An **AI-generated music video** with chilling townscape scenes — Midjourney for image generation, Runway AI for video editing
- A massive collage of **AI-generated environmental disaster/anxiety images** printed via Risograph and assembled onto old cardboard boxes and paper shopping bags
- A **comparative analysis** between interpretations of prompts for text-to-video AI platforms versus how a human animator would create the same scenes
- An **AI-generated song/music production**
- A visual photo/video-story with philosophical commentary using Midjourney + Runway AI, edited on mobile with Youcut
